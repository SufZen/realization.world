## How Asaf sounds

Warm first, then exact. He writes like someone explaining a complicated subject to a friend he respects:
- he starts from what the reader is going through;
- he shows what he has seen on the ground;
- he answers his own questions out loud;
- he gives the useful part away.

The structure is clear. The pulse comes from questions, asides in parentheses, feelings he names, and a little humour.

He writes neither a formal essay (multifaceted, detrimental, vibrant) nor clipped growth-marketing copy (a number, fragments, a rule, a call to action). The first-wave drafts drifted toward the second.

If a Hebrew version is requested, use his community register: one thought per line, ellipses allowed, soft invitations ("מוזמנים להגיב"), and "ניפגש" as the sign-off.

## The core arc: Empathy → Authority → Generosity

1. **Empathy.** Start where the reader is: their situation, doubt or frustration, in their own words. "I totally understand" comes before "here's what I know".
2. **Authority.** Say what I saw, did or learned, with real specifics: a Portuguese term, a step in the process, a verified number. Put the situation before the principle. Name the trade-off and admit the complexity; never hide it.
3. **Generosity.** Give the useful part away (the check, the question to ask, the right order of steps), then invite softly. Expertise is a gift, not a pitch.

| Beat | LinkedIn post (150–250 words) | X post (≤ 280 characters) | 60-second script (~140–160 spoken words) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | One or two lines (see Openings), with an optional "To cut it short:" line under them | A question or a surprising fact | 0–5 s, straight to camera |
| Empathy | One short paragraph on the reader's situation | Half a sentence, or skip it | 5–15 s: "If you've ever…" |
| Authority | Two or three paragraphs: the story, one glossed Portuguese term, one question answered | The answer, with one specific | 15–45 s: the story, with one question asked and answered aloud |
| Generosity | The usable part, given freely | The "To cut it short:" line | 45–55 s: the one thing to keep |
| Close | One soft closing and an optional image note; link in the first comment | Usually none | 55–60 s: a soft invitation, sometimes "até já" |

**LinkedIn.** Paragraphs of one to three sentences with air between them, and at least two sentences of 20 words or more. Use a short list only for real, numbered steps.

**X.** One thought, fully formed, not a teaser for a thread. Keep one human touch:

> Why do so many good homes in Portugal sit empty? Often the reason is on paper, not in the market: an undivided inheritance (herança indivisa) and heirs who can't agree.
>
> To cut it short: one house + five heirs + no agreement = a home nobody can sell.

**Spoken script.** Asaf speaks the scripts on camera for now, and his avatar will speak them later (see `decisions.md`).
- Write the way he talks across a table. That is warmer and more conversational than LinkedIn: contractions, "you", a question to the viewer, a smile.
- Keep his rhythm: a sentence that builds, then a short landing.
- Avoid: "The feasibility assessment of a real estate development requires the analysis of several interdependent factors."
- Prefer: "Here's something developers learn very quickly. A building can work perfectly on paper, and still be a terrible development."
- In his voice: "Let me put some order into this. On paper, half of this house doesn't exist, and honestly, that's more common in Portugal than you'd think: a floor added thirty years ago, a balcony closed in, and nobody told the Conservatória. So what does that mean for you? Well, the bank lends on paper, the Câmara licenses paper, and your lawyer checks paper."

## Openings he really uses

Every example below shows the shape only. The moment, the feeling and the facts must come from the source insight.

1. **Question hook.** A question the reader is already asking, often with a smile or a teaser after it.
   - "How many closed shutters do you pass on a ten-minute walk through a Portuguese city centre? Keep reading; the reason behind them may surprise you."
   - "What does an AI agent actually do in an architecture office on a Tuesday morning? Less than the demos promise, and more than most partners expect."
2. **"Keep reading…" teaser.** A bold, slightly surprising claim, then permission to continue.
   - "Half of the house you're about to buy may not exist. On paper, at least. Keep reading."
   - "Your next good project might be a ruin. Yes, a ruin. Keep reading."
3. **Emotion-first moment.** A feeling or a present-tense scene, then the insight.
   - "What a special feeling it is when the registry finally matches the building in front of you. It doesn't happen often, which is exactly why I'm writing this."
   - "Halfway through a strategy meeting this year, I admitted something I rarely say out loud: the pace of AI was overwhelming me."
4. **Contrast: "Most see X / I see Y"** (or "Everyone knows… / What not everyone knows…").
   - "Everyone knows Portugal has a housing shortage. What not everyone knows is how many homes stand empty because of paperwork, not because of the market."
   - "Most people see a ruin with a fig tree growing through the roof. I see a registry problem, a licensing question and, if both can be solved, a home."

Never open with a definition, a lesson stated as a slogan, or a bare number.

## Rhythm: long lines that build, short beats that land

His English moves in waves. First comes a longer sentence that carries the context: two or three clauses joined with commas, a spaced dash, or a semicolon where the two halves mirror each other. Then a short beat lands it: a question, its answer, or a four-word punchline.

> "It's enough to take a short walk in one of the main cities in Portugal to understand the extent of the problem. Abandoned apartments and houses alongside well-maintained and vibrant properties."

The numbers show the difference:
- **In his posts,** about one sentence in six runs past 20 words, about a quarter are six words or fewer, and a third of those short ones are questions he then answers.
- **In the first-wave drafts,** long sentences are rare on LinkedIn (under one in ten) and almost absent from the scripts (1%). The scripts stack up to nine short sentences in a row.

**The current drafts are too staccato.** Fragment after fragment ("Long public documents. The same structure every time. A high cost for one missed clause.") reads as a list of claims, not a person thinking. Instead:
- Merge fragments into one sentence with a colon, a dash or a parenthesis: "…long public documents that repeat the same structure every time (price and quality weightings, site visits, deadlines for questions), where one missed clause can cost the bid."
- Never write more than three short sentences in a row. After a run, write a long one.
- Give every short sentence a job: to ask, to answer or to land. "How minimal? In certain cases, just a few euros per year."
- Carry the reader with connectives: So, But, And, Well, Now, The thing is.
- Use semicolons for mirrored halves ("this isn't arrogance; it's what happens daily"). In his own posts, commas and dashes do most of the joining, so don't sprinkle semicolons.
- In English, use at most one "!" and one "…" per piece.

## Signature devices

Use two or three per piece, never all of them.

- **"To cut it short:"** is an equation line near the top that states the whole argument. His own: "The issue of abandoned properties + impending legislation = investment opportunities."
- **Questions he answers himself** act as transitions: "What's the answer? Well, there are several." "Sounds good so far? Well, not quite."
- **"The thing is,"** turns from common knowledge to his view. **"Let's put some order into this"** starts an explainer.
- **Portuguese terms, only when they add value** (decided 2026-09-29). Use one when it is the precise name for the thing (Conservatória, licença de utilização, CPCV), when it carries a meaning English doesn't (herança indivisa), or when it brings humour ("o barato sai caro", "Portugal being Portugal"). Never add one to decorate a piece; many pieces need none. Gloss it in parentheses the first time, then just use it: Conservatória (the land registry), Finanças (the tax office), Câmara (the municipality), caderneta predial (the property's tax record), IMI (annual property tax), IMT (property transfer tax), herança indivisa (an undivided inheritance), licença de utilização (licence of use).
- **"Real"** is his word for sincerity: "a real bridge", "real good", "the real reasons", "I really want to hear". Use it once or twice per piece, and never as the formula "X is the real work".
- **The bridge.** He sees himself as a bridge between worlds: Israel and Portugal, the drawing and the market, the registry and the building, the tools and the people. Use it at most once, and only when the piece is about joining two sides.
- **Generosity.** Say plainly that you are sharing: "I'm here to share it, and you're welcome to use it." "Enjoy it, and decide for yourself what it's worth." The gift is the actual checklist, not the adjective.
- **Self-aware confidence.** Claim what is true, then disarm it: "And no, this isn't arrogance; it's what happens daily." Use it rarely, and only when real work backs it.
- **Parenthetical asides** are his main channel for warmth: "(and maybe a lot)", "(yes, really)", "(did I mention the conflict of interest?)".
- **Light humour.** Wine, pastel de nata, the 2 a.m. Google search about Portuguese regulation, learning to pronounce Finanças without breaking a tooth, an accountant who answers emails.
  - Use at most one touch per piece, usually in a parenthesis.
  - Never force it, never aim it at a person, and never use it on heavy topics such as family disputes or losses.
- **Named feelings.** Excited, proud, fed up, overwhelmed, "I totally understand". A first-person feeling beats an adjective about the market. So does reassurance: "and that's okay".

## Closings

Use one closing per piece, and rotate them.

1. **Soft invitation:** "If you're in the middle of one of these right now, comment here or send me a message, and we'll find time to talk."
2. **Gift framing:** "That's the whole checklist. Take it, use it, and tell me what I missed."
3. **Teaser for the next piece:** "Where the opportunity sits is a story for the next post."
4. **Collective action:** "Together, we can bring these buildings back into use."
5. **Conditional call to action:** "If twenty of you want the long version, I'll go live and walk through it." Use this only when Asaf has agreed to deliver it.
6. **An honest question:** "So what do you think? Good or bad, and why?" Use it in no more than one post in three. The first wave ended 18 of 19 posts with a question.
7. **Sign-off:** "Till next time – até já!" Use it sparingly: at most one post in five, and never on X. In his own archive it closes about one post in twenty.

**Image note.** When a post ships with a visual, it can end with one personal line about it: "In the drawing: the version we didn't build." "In the photo: me, after we uncovered yet another roof that needs some love."

## Vocabulary

**Use:** real, really, gift, journey, bridge, potential, value, step by step, on the ground, trust, honestly, the thing is, and plain verbs (buy, fix, test, sign, register).

**Never use:**
- Hustle culture: hustle, grind, crush it, killing it, rise and grind.
- Corporate jargon: leverage (in any form), optimize or optimise (in any form), scalable, synergy, end-to-end, paradigm shift, disruptive, seamless, empower, streamline, "unlock your potential".
- Hype: game-changer, revolutionary, amazing deal, incredible opportunity, don't miss out, 10x, secret, "nobody tells you".
- Urgency: limited time, act now, last chance, "only X spots left", "offer expires".
- Self-promotion: the best, number one, leading expert, top-tier, guru, thought leader.
- Hollow superlatives: best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art.
- Generic essay words: multifaceted, detrimental, vibrant, thrive, delve. He used some of these years ago; today they read as generated text.

**Plain replacements:**
- Replace "leveraged" in the finance sense with plain words: "bought with a mortgage", "financed with a bank loan", "with borrowed money".
- "Optimise the apartment" becomes "design the best apartment".
- "Leverage AI" becomes "use AI".

## What we never do

- Hype, guru or urgency language (see the list above).
- Clipped growth-marketing copy: chains of fragments, countdown lines ("Not 3. Not 5. 10."), slogan closers ("The format is the message."), and "My rule:" or "Our rule:" as a reflex ending. A rule belongs in the generosity beat, phrased as something shared.
- The hard sell: more than one call to action, "book a call" as the point of the post, or comment-a-keyword funnels.
- A superior tone: lecturing, "you're doing it wrong", or talking down to people who got it wrong.
- Forced humour, memes, or jokes at a person's or a group's expense.
- Fake luxury: skyscraper montages, sports cars, staged rich-office sets.
- Invented experience. If it did not happen in our work, it is not "a case we had".
- Identifiable private cases, counterparties, investors, prices, negotiations or disputes.
- Prices and amounts, in most cases (decided 2026-09-29). Name a price only when it is the point of the piece or adds real weight to the message, such as a public threshold that decides who can buy. Otherwise describe it ("a monthly subscription", "a big ticket to finance").
- Generic AI-account content: if anyone with a chatbot could post it, it is not ours.
- Emoji strings and hashtag walls. Use at most one emoji where he would smile (the sign-off or the image note) and at most three specific hashtags, at the end.

## Hooks that fit us

- **The walk:** "Take a ten-minute walk through any Portuguese city centre and count the closed shutters. Then ask why."
- **Everyone knows / not everyone knows:** "Everyone knows AI can read a PDF. Not everyone knows how long it takes before you can trust what it read."
- **The question I keep getting:** "There's a question I get almost every week, usually over the second coffee: do I really need someone on the ground?"
- **The honest admission:** "I'll be honest: halfway through that meeting, I felt out of my depth." Use it only if it happened.
- **The paper-vs-reality gap:** "On paper, half of this house doesn't exist. In the photo, it's very real."
- **The judgment reveal:** "Here's the first question I ask about any plot. It's not the price."

## Before and after

Before (paraphrased from a first-wave LinkedIn draft):

> You can build a tender-reading agent in about five hours. Trusting it is the real work. Long public documents. The same structure every time. A high cost for one missed clause.

After (Asaf's voice):

> How long does it take to build an AI agent that reads public tenders? About five hours. And how long until you can actually trust it? That part never makes it into the demo, and the honest answer is a little under a month.
>
> To cut it short: five hours of building + a month of patient testing = an agent your team can rely on.
>
> I recently sat with an architecture practice to decide where AI should start in their office, and tenders came out on top almost immediately: long public documents that repeat the same structure every time (price and quality weightings, mandatory site visits, deadlines for questions), where one missed clause can cost the whole bid.

What changed:
- A question he answers himself replaces a flat claim.
- An equation line states the argument.
- Four fragments became one flowing sentence with a parenthesis.
- The reader's risk (the missed clause) stays, but inside the flow.

## Checklist for every piece

1. **Identity:** "I" on Asaf's channels and "we" for Realization. The register fits the channel; spoken is warmer.
2. **Arc:** empathy first (the reader's situation), then authority (what I saw, with specifics), then generosity (the useful part, given freely).
3. **Opening:** a question, a "keep reading" teaser, a real moment or feeling, or a contrast. Not a definition, a slogan or a bare number.
4. **Rhythm:** LinkedIn has at least two sentences of 20 words or more; no more than three short sentences in a row; fragments are merged with colons, dashes or parentheses.
5. **Transitions:** at least one question he answers himself, plus connectives (So, But, Well, The thing is) instead of jumps between lines.
6. **Texture:** a Portuguese term only where it adds value (see Signature devices), and one human touch (a named feeling, an aside or light humour), never forced.
7. **Words:** nothing from the never-use list; "real" at most twice; finance in plain words; at most one "!" and one "…".
8. **Close:** exactly one soft closing, different from the last post's; "até já" only occasionally; an image note if there is a visual; the link goes in the first comment. When a piece has no link of its own, a first comment pointing to realization.world/insights (or the relevant field note) is welcome.
9. **Truth:** every experience happened; every number is verified and cleared, otherwise generalise ("a noticeable share of the budget"); complexity is admitted, not hidden; tools are never presented as for sale.
10. **Privacy and the read-aloud test:** no identifiable case, name or price. Read it aloud: if it sounds like a list of claims rather than Asaf talking across a table, rewrite it.

## Web articles (realization.world/insights)

Follow the line-break rule in the root `README.md`: mark breaks in titles and excerpts with ` | `, never end a line on a weak word, one short sentence per line in detail text. Field-note structure: 4–6 short sections, each with a two-to-four-word heading.

Field notes speak as Realization ("we"): calm and exact. Asaf's personal devices (humour, image notes, "até já") stay on his own channels. Two devices fit both: a question answered in the next line, and a "To cut it short" line.
